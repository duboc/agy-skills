# Key UX Laws & Usability Heuristics

Reference these established UX laws and heuristics to justify design critique recommendations with objective authority.

---

## Psychological & UX Laws

### 1. Fitts's Law
- **Principle**: The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target.
- **UX Application**: Make primary interactive elements (buttons, links) large enough and place them close to expected cursor/thumb areas.
- **Red Flag**: Tiny clickable icons placed far apart or in hard-to-reach screen corners.

### 2. Hick's Law
- **Principle**: The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices.
- **UX Application**: Simplify choices for the user by breaking complex processes into steps (progressive disclosure) and highlighting recommended defaults.
- **Red Flag**: Overwhelming navigation menus, cluttered pricing tables, or screens with 5+ competing actions.

### 3. Jakob's Law
- **Principle**: Users spend most of their time on other sites. This means that users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know.
- **UX Application**: Use familiar design patterns (e.g., shopping cart icon top-right, logo top-left returns home, standard search bar patterns).
- **Red Flag**: Reinventing standard interaction controls unnecessarily (e.g., custom non-standard scrollbars or unorthodox form inputs).

### 4. Miller's Law
- **Principle**: The average person can only keep 7 (plus or minus 2) items in their working memory.
- **UX Application**: Chunk information into digestible groups (e.g., formatting phone numbers as `(555) 000-0000`, grouping menu items into labeled sections).
- **Red Flag**: Long unformatted lists, massive blocks of text without headers or bullet points.

## Gestalt Principles of Perception

1. **Law of Proximity**: Objects that are close to each other tend to be perceived as a single group.
   - *Application*: Keep form field labels close to their respective inputs and separate distinct form sections with larger spatial margins.
2. **Law of Similarity**: Elements that share visual characteristics (color, shape, size) are perceived as related or having identical functions.
   - *Application*: Ensure all primary buttons look identical and all clickable links share the same color/underline convention.
3. **Law of Continuity**: The eye naturally follows lines, paths, and curves.
   - *Application*: Align form fields and content cards along strong vertical grid lines for effortless vertical scanning.
4. **Law of Focal Point**: Whatever stands out visually will capture and hold the viewer's attention first.
   - *Application*: Use contrasting primary colors sparingly for main actions so they pop against neutral structural backgrounds.

## Nielsen Norman Group 10 Usability Heuristics Summary

1. **Visibility of System Status**: Always keep users informed about what is happening through appropriate feedback within reasonable time.
2. **Match Between System and Real World**: Use words, phrases, and concepts familiar to the user rather than internal jargon.
3. **User Control and Freedom**: Provide clear "emergency exits" (undo, cancel, close buttons).
4. **Consistency and Standards**: Words, situations, and actions should mean the same thing across the product.
5. **Error Prevention**: Prevent problems from occurring in the first place through smart defaults and constraints.
6. **Recognition Rather Than Recall**: Minimize user memory load by making objects, actions, and options visible.
7. **Flexibility and Efficiency of Use**: Accelerators (keyboard shortcuts, touch gestures) for expert users while keeping simple paths for novices.
8. **Aesthetic and Minimalist Design**: Interfaces should not contain information that is irrelevant or rarely needed.
9. **Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors**: Error messages expressed in plain language, indicating the problem and constructively suggesting a solution.
10. **Help and Documentation**: Easy to search, focused on user tasks, listing concrete steps.
