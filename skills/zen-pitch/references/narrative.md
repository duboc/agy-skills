## The three-act spine

Every arc in the catalog is a variation on the same underlying shape:

**Tension** — establish that a real problem exists, in the audience's own terms, with a number they recognize.
**Pivot** — a single slide that turns the deck. Almost empty. Usually a question.
**Resolution** — how it gets solved, what changes, what it means.

Proportions that work: roughly 40% tension, one slide of pivot, 55% resolution. Decks skew short on tension because the author already believes the problem is real. The audience does not yet, and a resolution nobody feels the need for lands flat.

The tension act is also where you earn the right to talk about your solution. Skip it and the deck reads as a product pitch from slide 2.

## Arc catalog

Pick the shape that matches the situation, not the one that sounds most impressive.

### 1. Problem / Solution
The default. Establish pain, quantify it, pivot, resolve.

**Use when:** the audience already feels the pain but has not sized it.
**Shape:** context → the multiplication of the problem → the number → what it costs → *pivot* → what changed → the mechanism → what it changes → close.
**Failure mode:** spending too long on the problem with an audience that lives it daily. If they are nodding by slide 3, cut a tension slide.

### 2. Before / After / Bridge
Show the current state, show the desired state, then reveal the bridge.

**Use when:** the destination is more compelling than the problem — transformation stories, migrations, rebuilds.
**Shape:** here is today → here is what good looks like → *pivot: what stands between* → the bridge → the first step.
**Failure mode:** the "after" sounds like marketing. Anchor it in something concrete and specific, ideally something the audience can picture on a Tuesday afternoon.

### 3. Contrarian reversal
State the conventional wisdom, then dismantle it.

**Use when:** the audience holds a belief that blocks the solution, and you have real evidence against it.
**Shape:** everyone believes X → here is why X made sense → here is what changed → *pivot: X is now the expensive choice* → what replaces it → what it costs to keep believing X.
**Failure mode:** attacking a straw man. If the conventional wisdom you name is not one the room actually holds, the deck reads as condescending. Only use this when you can state the opposing view better than its holders would.

### 4. Escalating stakes
Each act raises what is at risk.

**Use when:** the problem compounds — technical debt, security, compliance, anything where the cost of inaction grows.
**Shape:** the small version → what it becomes at scale → what it becomes if ignored → *pivot* → where to intervene → the cost of intervening now versus later.
**Failure mode:** tipping into fear-mongering. Escalate with evidence at each level, and be honest about which level is speculative.

### 5. Origin story
Start with a specific moment, generalize outward.

**Use when:** the deck is about something you built or lived, and the specificity is the credibility.
**Shape:** a specific concrete moment → why it happened → why it happens everywhere → *pivot* → what we built → what it means generally.
**Failure mode:** the audience does not care about your story. Cut to the generalization faster than feels natural.

### 6. Comparison
Two or three approaches, evaluated against shared criteria.

**Use when:** the audience is choosing between real options and wants to feel the decision was theirs.
**Shape:** the decision at hand → the criteria that matter → option A → option B → *pivot: what the criteria actually reward* → the recommendation → what would change it.
**Failure mode:** rigging the comparison. If one option is obviously a placeholder, the whole deck loses credibility. Steelman every option, and name the conditions under which the losing option would win.

## Writing slide statements

The unit of a Zen deck is the sentence on the slide. Everything else is support.

**A slide statement makes a claim.** Not a topic, not a label, not a category name.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Market challenges | The calendar doesn't wait for the creative to be ready |
| Scaling issues | One idea doesn't become an ad. It becomes a matrix. |
| Our approach | The spec goes into the prompt, not into the cut |
| Benefits | The machine takes the matrix. The team goes back to the idea. |

**Craft notes:**

- **Two lines, hard maximum.** A statement that needs three lines is two slides or a worse statement.
- **Prefer concrete nouns.** "Files, deadlines, rejections" beats "operational inefficiencies."
- **Use the sentence break as rhythm.** Two short sentences with a period between them read like speech and land harder than one long clause. "One idea doesn't become an ad. It becomes a matrix."
- **Avoid the colon-subtitle pattern.** "Scaling Creative: A New Approach" is a report heading, not a slide.
- **No accent line under the statement.** Whitespace does that job.
- **One number per slide, at most.** Two numbers on a slide means the audience picks one and misses the other.

## The pivot slide

The pivot is the highest-leverage slide in the deck and the one most often cut for being "empty."

**Anatomy:** dark background, the visual motif, one sentence, nothing else. No supporting line, no chart, no footer beyond the deck signature.

**It is usually a question.** "What if adapting stopped being work?" A question makes the audience answer it internally, which is what makes the resolution feel earned rather than sold.

**It can also be a reversal** — a flat statement that inverts what the tension act established. "The bottleneck was never creativity."

The pivot works because of contrast. If the deck already uses dark slides for content, the pivot loses its force — reserve dark for the cover, the pivot, and the close.

Write the speaker note explicitly telling the presenter to pause here. Presenters rush empty slides because empty slides feel uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the point.

## Landing the ending

The closing slide returns to the opening tension. If slide 4 established 80%, the close is about that 80%.

**Endings that work:**
- The reversal of the opening number — "the team goes back to the idea"
- The single implication the audience should carry out of the room
- The one decision you are asking for, stated plainly

**Endings that do not:**
- A summary of what was just said. The audience was there.
- A new idea. It reopens the deck instead of closing it.
- "Thank you" or "Questions?" as the only content. Put the closing statement on the slide and say thank you out loud.
- A wall of contact details. One line at the bottom is enough.

## Speaker notes

In a Zen deck the slides are nearly empty, so the notes are where the argument lives. A deck with good slides and no notes is unusable by anyone except the person who built it — and barely usable by them a month later.

**Write notes as direction to a presenter, not as a transcript.** A transcript gets read aloud, badly. Direction gets internalized.

What belongs in a note:

- **The point of this slide in one line** — why it exists in the arc
- **The move** — pause here, let this sit, go fast through this one
- **The vulnerability** — which number will draw a challenge and how to handle it. "These are orders of magnitude, not a contractual promise — present them that way."
- **The concession** — what to grant before being asked. Conceding early buys credibility for the rest.
- **The callback** — where this connects to an earlier or later slide

Length: two to four sentences. Notes long enough to read on stage are notes nobody reads.

**Example of a note that earns its place:**

> These are orders of magnitude, not contractual promises — present them that way. The last row is the one that matters to whoever controls budget: this isn't about producing more cheaply, it's about reallocating the most expensive talent. Expect a challenge on the second row; concede that it depends on how many placements are in scope.

## Presenting the arc for approval

Show the arc as a numbered list, one line per slide, each line stating the claim. Nothing else — no rationale paragraph, no design notes. The user is evaluating whether the story holds, and anything else is noise at that moment.

After the list, ask three things in one short paragraph: does the arc hold, what is missing, what should be cut.

**Do not defend the arc when they push back.** They know the audience and you do not. Revise, show the revision, move on. If a cut genuinely breaks the arc, say what breaks and offer a replacement rather than arguing for the original.
