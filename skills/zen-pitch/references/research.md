# Research

How to research a domain until it yields a deck rather than a summary.

## What you are looking for

A summary answers "what is true about this space." A deck needs something narrower: **the number that makes the audience recognize their own situation.**

That number has three properties:

1. **Specific.** "80% of creative-team effort goes to adaptation" — not "a lot of time is wasted."
2. **Recognizable.** The audience should react with "yes, that's us," not "huh, interesting." A statistic about the industry at large is weaker than one about the daily experience of the person in the room.
3. **Sourced or clearly labeled.** State where it came from, or state that it is an order of magnitude. Presenting an estimate as a finding is the fastest way to lose a room.

Everything else in the research serves this number: what causes it, what it costs, what recently changed to make it addressable.

---

## The layered search pattern

Four layers. Each one narrows.

**Layer 1 — the domain.** Establish vocabulary and the shape of the space. One or two searches. Mostly you are learning what the practitioners call things, which makes every later query better.

**Layer 2 — the specific pain.** Now that you have the vocabulary, search the actual friction. Not "content marketing" but "creative production bottleneck multi-platform."

**Layer 3 — quantification.** This is where most research stops too early. Search explicitly for numbers: time studies, benchmark reports, survey data, cost breakdowns, practitioner accounts with figures in them. Try several phrasings — the number often lives in a report summary or a vendor benchmark rather than in an article.

**Layer 4 — what changed.** A deck needs a reason the problem is solvable *now*. New capability, new pricing, new regulation, new tooling. Without this layer the deck answers "why has nobody done this," and badly.

Then one more round on whatever is still thin. Research that feels complete after four layers usually has a hole in the quantification layer.

---

## Recency discipline

Anything that looks like a spec, limit, price, capability, or org chart goes stale, and a deck that states a stale figure gets corrected in the room — which costs the whole deck its credibility, not just that slide.

Verify:
- Platform limits, file specs, API constraints
- Pricing and tiers
- Product capabilities and model names
- Who holds a role, who owns a company
- Regulatory deadlines and effective dates

Do not verify: durable concepts, historical facts, mechanisms, math.

When a source is more than about a year old on a fast-moving topic, either find a newer one or say on the slide when the figure is from. Dated-but-labeled beats current-sounding-but-wrong.

---

## Deck-worthiness filter

Gather far more than you will use — perhaps 30 facts for a deck that will show 8. Then filter. A fact earns a slide if it does one of these:

- **Establishes the problem is real** (tension act)
- **Sizes the problem** (the number)
- **Explains why it persists** (why smart people have not fixed it)
- **Marks what changed** (the pivot's justification)
- **Demonstrates the mechanism works** (resolution act)
- **Quantifies the delta** (the close)

Facts that do none of these are speaker-note material. They are still worth having — most hard questions from an audience are answered from the notes, not the slides.

---

## Handling contested facts

If sources disagree, say so on the slide or in the notes. A deck that acknowledges a contested number is more credible than one that picks a side silently, and the acknowledgment usually pre-empts the objection.

If a number is your own estimate rather than a source's, label it as an order of magnitude and put a line in the speaker notes telling the presenter to frame it that way out loud. This is the single most common way a good deck loses a room: an estimate presented in the same voice as a finding, challenged, and unable to be defended.

---

## Mining the conversation first

Often the research already exists — the user pasted a spec, or an earlier turn in the session produced it. Mine that before searching. Then search only to:

- Verify anything time-sensitive in the existing material
- Fill the quantification layer, which existing material usually lacks
- Find the "what changed" layer, which is almost never in a spec document

Re-researching from scratch when the material is already in the conversation wastes time and tends to produce a deck that contradicts what the user already believes.

---

## From research to spine

Once research is done, write the spine before writing the narrative:

```
Challenge element  |  Evidence  |  What resolves it  |  Slide-worthy?
```

The spine does two jobs. It filters — most rows are not slide-worthy. And it keeps the argument honest: a row with a challenge and evidence but an empty "what resolves it" column is a real gap, and the deck should acknowledge it rather than route around it. Audiences trust decks that name their own limits.
